## Abstract

Interpretive stability in prior-guided spectral attention is usually reported as the overlap of the top-k selected bands across runs. We disentangle prior-based gate initialisation from prior-based regularisation and examine their distinct effects on learned spectral selections. Initialising at a step-function prior over 281 near-infrared bands, from our own earlier work, leaves the prior's saturated level operationally inert: the gradient there is of order 10^-6 and the penalty cannot move it. That geometry creates the possibility of an artefact but does not produce one; three conditions together do, and we separate them: an oversized level set, ties that persist through training, and a deterministic rule that resolves them. The near-infrared prior meets all three; an overlap of 0.9418, at that codebase's convention of q = 28, falls to 0.7005 +- 0.0068 under random tie resolution, against an exact expectation of 0.7012 for drawing 28 of 34 plateau bands twice. A continuous Raman prior meets none. Outside the saturated plateau, the gates retained data-associated structure, including positive alignment with a separate statistical reference across the 223 floor-level bands. A displacement-based control in that earlier study depends on neither the top-k selection nor the tie-breaking and is immune to this artefact; excluding the plateau bands lowers it by 0.018 (Pearson) or 0.051 (Spearman) at the largest sample size, and what survives still responds to n. For identical stored gates, at the revision data's own convention of q = 29, the overlap ranged from 0.7443-0.7659 under one sorting implementation to 0.9452-0.9867 under another, and reached 1.0000 with index-based tie-breaking; when the selection boundary intersects a tied group, the overlap reflects the complete selection procedure rather than an identifiable property of the gates. We report twenty pre-registered hypotheses, eight of which our criteria did not support; the tie-breaking analysis was a post hoc diagnostic, reported separately from those verdicts.

## 1. Introduction

Spectral models that expose a per-band gate are attractive because the gate
invites a reading: the bands it raises are the bands the model found
informative. When a prior derived from the literature is used to guide that
gate, the reading acquires a second claim — that the guidance makes the
selection reproducible, so that independent runs identify the same chemistry.
The usual evidence for this claim is the overlap of the top-k gated bands
across runs that differ only in their random seed, reported as a Jaccard index
and compared, when it is compared at all, against an intuition of what chance
would give.

This paper examines the conditions under which that overlap means what it is
taken to mean. The conditions are geometric, they are checkable before any
model is trained, and the way of encoding a prior that we used in our own
earlier work violates them. How often other encodings do is not something we
measured, and we do not assert it.

### 1.1 What the interpretive claim would have to assert

It helps to state the two claims in the terms a learning problem provides,
because they are not the same kind of claim and only one of them has an
established estimand.

The predictive claim is standard. A hypothesis class, a loss, and a
distribution over spectra and reference values define a risk; the empirical
risk on held-out data estimates it; and uniform convergence over the class
bounds the gap between the two as a function of sample size and the class's
capacity. The quantity being estimated exists independently of the estimator,
and the guarantee is about approaching it.

The interpretive claim has no such structure. To say that a procedure
*identifies* informative bands presupposes a target set of bands — a true
support — and asserts that the procedure estimates it. Agreement between two
runs then measures the variance of that estimator and says nothing about its
bias: two runs can agree perfectly and both be wrong, and they will do so
whenever the agreement is produced by something the two runs share that is not
the data. A prior shared by both runs is exactly such a thing.

We do not claim that seed overlap has no population limit. Under assumptions
about algorithmic stability, the data distribution and asymptotic behaviour, a
selection overlap may well converge to a well-defined population quantity, and
nothing here rules that out. The narrower point is the one the argument needs:
**even where the overlap converges, its limit is a property of the procedure's
agreement with itself, and convergence to it does not establish that the
selection recovers a true support.** Held-out risk differs not in having a
limit but in what the limit is — a property of the data distribution, against
which the estimate can be wrong in a way that more data corrects. Reproducibility is therefore necessary for recovery of a support and
nowhere near sufficient, and the gap between the two is where a prior can do
its work undetected.

We do not argue that the interpretive claim is unavailable. We argue that the
measure in common use does not test it, and we give the conditions under which
it would.

### 1.2 Restricted reachability is not restricted capacity

Part of our account turns on gate parameters that gradient descent cannot
move, and it is worth separating that from a claim we are not making.

Initialising the gate at the prior places some parameters in a region where the
gradient of the training objective with respect to them is numerically
negligible. Those parameters are then effectively frozen: the fitted model
occupies a proper subset of the parameter space, and the bands they control
contribute a value fixed by the prior rather than learned. This is a statement
about which hypotheses the optimiser can reach from a particular starting
point.

It is not a statement about the hypothesis class. The class is the same
parametric family either way, with the same capacity by any of the usual
measures; nothing about the initialisation removes a function from it or
changes what it could express. In particular, no hierarchy of nested classes is
induced, and the apparatus of structural risk minimisation does not apply. The
restriction lives in the optimisation, not in the class, and the distinction
matters because the two have different remedies: a capacity restriction would
be a modelling choice to defend, whereas a reachability restriction is an
artefact of initialisation and is removed by changing it, which is what we
recommend.

### 1.3 What this paper does and does not conclude

We separate three conditions that are easily conflated, show which of them a
step-function prior satisfies, demonstrate the consequence on an archived
result, and verify on a second modality with a continuous prior that the
consequence is not intrinsic to the architecture. We then report where the gate
does and does not follow the data.

That last point should be stated at the outset, so that the paper is not read
as a denial of it. **The gate is not inert.** On both modalities it aligns with
a separate, data-derived statistical reference, the alignment is positive in
every
partition we measured, and it survives conditioning on the prior. What we find
is that this alignment is independent of which prior was used, and that it is
present at the prior's lowest level, which carries 223 of the 281 bands and
which the top-decile metric never reads. We do not claim it is *absent* at the
prior's maximum: the measurements there are inconsistent in sign and we
describe them as such, without converting a weaker result into a demonstrated
difference between the two levels (Section 8.1). The gate learns from the data;
the top-decile overlap, in the configuration examined here, is not where that
learning can be seen. Section 8 reports it.

## 2. Data and methods

### 2.1 Datasets

**Near-infrared.** The mango dry-matter dataset, version 3, harvest 4
(DOI 10.17632/46htwnp833.3), with p = 281 wavelengths. Splits are grouped by
fruit: the collection holds 725 fruits of which 723 were measured twice, so a
random split over spectra would place two measurements of one fruit on opposite
sides of the partition and inflate held-out performance. All partitions
reported here are grouped.

**Fruit identity is inferred, not supplied, and we state what that does and
does not guarantee.** The published file carries no fruit identifier. The
grouping variable is constructed as runs of consecutive rows sharing every
metadata field and the dry-matter value, and whole groups are assigned to one
side of a split. We verified three properties over all fifteen split seeds used
in this work and the one default. The group-size distribution reproduces the
recorded structure exactly: 723 groups of two spectra and 2 of one, 725 groups
over 1448 rows. No metadata-and-dry-matter key spans more than one group. And
in every split, no such key appears on both sides.

The third property is strong evidence of per-fruit separation and not a proof
of it. Concluding that no physical fruit straddles a split requires the further
assumption that every measurement of one fruit carries an identical key, which
is the assumption the identification procedure rests on; the check is therefore
circular with respect to it, and the file holds no fruit identifier with which
to break the circle. This is a limitation of the source data rather than of the
splitting procedure, and we report the verified properties rather than the
conclusion they would support under the extra assumption.

**Raman.** The `bioprocess_substrates` collection, glucose as the regression
target, with p = 1870 Raman shifts. The source records 6960 spectra; 488 carry
no glucose value and are dropped at ingestion, leaving 6472 analysed. The
exclusion criterion is a missing (`NaN`) target and nothing else, and the count
is recorded by the loader rather than reconstructed afterwards. Splits are not
grouped: the ingestion exposes no batch identifier, so no grouping variable is
available, and this is a limitation rather than a choice (Section 10).

### 2.2 Priors

The near-infrared prior is assembled by assigning one constant value to each
reported absorption region — 0.6 to 680–700 nm (chlorophyll), 1.0 to
900–1000 nm (second C–H overtone), 0.8 to 1100–1200 nm (first C–H overtone) —
over a floor of 0.1 elsewhere, and is then divided by its maximum. On the
281-wavelength axis this gives four distinct values, 0.1 on 223 bands, 0.6 on
7, 0.8 on 17, and 1.0 on the 34 bands falling inside the 900–1000 nm window,
which is 12.1% of the spectrum.

It is worth being exact about where the plateau comes from, because the
generality of what follows depends on it. **The plateau is a property of the
construction, not of the normalisation.** Division by the maximum is in fact
the identity map here, since the largest assigned value is already 1; all it
would ever do is fix the top level. The block of 34 numerically equal entries
exists because a single constant was assigned to a region 100 nm wide. Any
prior written in the form *these regions matter this much* has level sets as
large as the regions it names, and that is the ordinary way to encode published
band assignments. The condition examined in this paper is therefore not a quirk
of one normalisation scheme: it is violated whenever the largest named region
contains more bands than the selected set.

The Raman prior is a sum of Gaussians centred on reported glucose bands, with a
width of 15 cm⁻¹, peak-normalised and clipped below at 0.01. It takes 545
distinct values over 1870 shifts and attains its maximum at a single band. The
two priors encode comparable chemical knowledge and differ in quantisation, and
that difference is what makes the Raman arm a control rather than a replication.

### 2.3 Gate and objective

The gate is a per-band sigmoid, `g = σ(θ)`, multiplying the spectrum before a
light multilayer perceptron, trained end to end. Two ways of letting the prior
`s` enter are compared throughout and must not be confused:

- **prior in the initialisation**, `θ₀ = logit(s)`, under which the prior sets
  the starting point and no penalty is applied;
- **penalty only**, `θ₀ = 0` with a term `λ · KL(g ‖ s)` summed over bands,
  under which the prior constrains the solution without placing any parameter
  at the start.

The second is what we recommend, and the distinction is the subject of
Section 3.

### 2.4 Selection metric, and the two decile conventions in use

Interpretive stability is reported as the mean pairwise Jaccard index of the
top-q gated bands across seeds. The definition of q is not uniform across the
codebases this paper draws on, and because the two definitions give different
numbers and different chance levels, **we declare both and never mix them**:

| source | definition of q | q at p = 281 | exact E[J] on a 34-band plateau |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| this work, and the stored metrics of the earlier revision | `ceil(0.10 p)` | 29 | 0.7445 |
| the published ablation of the earlier study | `floor(p / 10)` | 28 | 0.7012 |

Every selection figure below carries the convention of the code that produced
it. Figures taken from the published ablation are reported at q = 28 and
compared against 0.7012; figures recomputed on the stored gate vectors of the
earlier revision are reported at q = 29 and compared against 0.7445.

### 2.5 The chance level, and a correction to how it is usually computed

A chance level for a Jaccard index over a tied block of L bands is often
quoted as q / (2L − q). That expression is not the expected Jaccard index. If
two sets of size q are drawn uniformly and independently from L bands, their
overlap k is hypergeometric with mean q²/L, and

J(q²/L) = (q²/L) / (2q − q²/L) = q / (2L − q)

identically: the familiar ratio is J evaluated at the expected overlap. But
J(k) = k/(2q − k) has J″(k) = 4q/(2q − k)³ > 0 for k < 2q, so J is strictly
convex in k and Jensen's inequality gives E[J] > J(E[k]). The ratio is a strict
lower bound. We therefore report the exact expectation,

E[J] = Σₖ [C(q,k) · C(L−q, q−k) / C(L,q)] · k / (2q − k),

which exceeds the ratio by 0.0012 at q = 28 and by 0.0009 at q = 29 for
L = 34. The gap is smaller than the sampling spread of the estimates reported
below and changes none of our verdicts, but it is systematic and in the
direction that overstates any excess above chance, so it should not be carried
into a comparison that is closer than ours.

### 2.6 External reference

To ask whether the gate follows the data, we need a reference that the gate did
not help produce. We use the absolute regression coefficients of a partial
least squares model fitted on the training partition only. The number of latent
components H is chosen by the one-standard-error rule on a cross-validated
error curve computed within the training partition — grouped by fruit for the
near-infrared data, and over plain folds for Raman, where no grouping variable
exists — with the search capped at 120 components and further capped by the
fold sizes. The reference is recomputed per partition and never shared across
partitions, and the gate plays no part in producing it.

The selected H is 46 to 72 across the fifteen near-infrared partitions, and 5,
6 and 6 across the three Raman partitions. The contrast is worth noting before
any comparison between the modalities: a reference built from tens of
components and one built from five or six are not equally sharp localisations
of the signal, and Section 10 returns to what that limits.

### 2.7 Pre-registration

**What a seed test and a partition count can each support.** Two units of
replication appear below and they answer different questions. A test across the
ten random initialisations within one partition characterises the variability
of the optimisation procedure *conditional on that partition's training data*,
which all ten initialisations share; it does not by itself support
population-level inference about spectral relevance. A count across partitions
describes how a measurement behaves under re-splitting, and the partitions are
repeated 80/20 splits of the same 725 fruits with approximately 80% overlap
between training sets, so they are not independent replicates either. We report
seed tests as statements about run-to-run stability, partition counts as
descriptions, and no test over partitions as population inference.

Every hypothesis reported in this paper was written down, with its acceptance
criterion, and committed before the runs that evaluate it. Criteria were not
amended after the data were seen; where a criterion turned out to measure
something other than what it was written to measure, the original text is kept
and the misreading is reported alongside the verdict. Table 1 lists all twenty
hypotheses with their verdicts and, in a separate column, the interpretation we
reached afterwards. Eight were not supported.

**The tie-breaking analysis was not part of the original pre-registered
hypothesis set.** It was introduced as a post hoc diagnostic investigation
after the observed stability patterns raised questions about the
interpretation of top-q overlap. Accordingly, the results of this analysis are
reported separately from the pre-registered hypothesis verdicts in Table 1.
The computational dependence of top-q selection on boundary ties follows from
the selection procedure itself; its observed magnitude and consequences are
specific to the experimental settings examined here. We state this before the
results rather than in the discussion, because a reader is entitled to know
which claims were committed in advance and which were not before reading
either.

## 3. Results — the two roles of the prior

A prior can enter this model in two ways, and the distinction is usually
treated as an implementation detail. It is not: one of the two restricts which
gate configurations the optimiser can reach, and the other does not.

### 3.1 Initialising at the prior freezes the plateau

Under `θ₀ = logit(s)`, the 34 bands at the prior's maximum start at a gate
value of 1 and stay there. The gradient of any loss with respect to θ carries a
factor σ′(θ) = g(1 − g), which at the plateau is of order 10⁻⁶; the Fisher
information for those coordinates is 8.6 × 10⁻⁹ against 0.72 for the bands off
the plateau, a ratio of about eight orders of magnitude. The parameters are
free in the class and unreachable by the optimiser, which is the distinction
drawn in Section 1.2.

The consequence is visible in how little the penalty weight then matters. With
the prior in the initialisation, ρ(gate, prior) stays between 0.9987 and 0.9999
across four orders of magnitude of λ: the gate is pinned by where it started,
and the term that was supposed to control how strongly the prior acts is
inert.

### 3.2 Separating the two roles removes the restriction

Setting θ₀ = 0 and letting the prior act only through the penalty removes the
saturation entirely. The count of inert bands — bands whose gate cannot move —
is zero at every λ across seven grids, and zero across all 1500 runs of the
five partitions used for the controls. The prior still constrains the solution;
it no longer decides which solutions are available.

This is the first of our recommendations and the cheapest: the prior's
constraint and the prior's starting point are separable, and separating them
costs nothing in predictive performance (Section 5) while removing the
condition that the rest of this paper is about.

## 4. What the top-decile overlap measures

### 4.1 The prior's geometry

The near-infrared literature prior is a step function with four distinct values
over the 281 retained bands: 0.1 on 223 bands, 0.6 on 7, 0.8 on 17, and 1.0 on
34. The elevated regions correspond to published assignments — the chlorophyll
band near 690 nm, the second C–H overtone near 950 nm, and the first overtone
near 1150 nm — and the remaining bands carry a floor.

Top-k overlap is evaluated on the top decile. Two conventions for q coexist in
this program's codebase and give different values at p = 281:
`ceil(0.10 p) = 29` and floor division `p // 10 = 28`. We state which applies
to each dataset throughout and never combine them, because the reference levels
differ.

### 4.2 Three conditions, and what each one costs

A plateau larger than q does not by itself make a stability figure artificial.
The effect reported here requires three conditions, and they are separable. We
state them in order of what they cost the metric, and our data exhibit the
three combinations that occur.

**(C1) The plateau exceeds q.** Let L be the number of bands the prior places at
its maximum and suppose the trained gate is larger on all of them than on any
band outside. Then the top-q set is a subset of the plateau whenever L ≥ q, and
the metric never examines a band outside the prior's top level, whatever the
model has learned. Here L = 34 against q = 28 or 29.

C1 alone makes the metric **blind**, not artificial. With the prior moved out of
the initialisation (θ₀ = 0), the gate on the plateau is continuous, no two
values coincide, and the top-q set is chosen by magnitude: the overlap is then a
genuine agreement about which of the 34 bands rank highest. We observe exactly
this in a pre-registered sweep of three prior arms over five grouped
partitions — the decile lies inside the plateau in 15 of 15 cells, and no cell
contains an exact tie among trained gate values.

**(C2) The ties persist after training.** When the prior also initialises the
gate logits, θ₀ = logit(s), the gradient reaching θ scales with
σ′(θ) = s(1 − s), so bands with s = 1.0 begin where that factor is of order
10⁻⁶ and do not move within the budget. In the archived gate of the earlier
study the 34 plateau bands then hold one value to the last bit (0.99999380;
spread 0.0 × 10⁰; mean s(1 − s) = 6.2 × 10⁻⁶), and every band outside lies
strictly below.

**(C3) The tie is resolved by the sort.** With C1 and C2, the choice of q bands
from L is not made by magnitude, and the reported figure depends on the rule
that breaks the tie.

Under a rule that resolves ties at random, the selected set is uniform over the
C(L, q) subsets of the plateau. For two independent runs, |A ∩ B| is
hypergeometric and the expected overlap is

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;E[J] = Σₖ [ C(q,k) C(L−q, q−k) / C(L,q) ] · k / (2q − k),

which is **0.7012** for L = 34, q = 28 and **0.7445** for L = 34, q = 29. The
familiar approximation q/(2L − q) gives 0.7000 and 0.7436, within 0.0012 of the
exact values.

Under the deterministic rule actually used, `numpy.argsort` is not a stable
sort, so the order within a tied block follows the partition, which is a
function of the whole array — including the values the metric does not select.
The figure is therefore neither 1 (which an index-ordered rule would give) nor
the chance level. Holding the plateau as trained and perturbing only the
remaining bands, the overlap falls from 1.0000 at zero perturbation to 0.9678
at sd = 10⁻⁴ and 0.7500 at sd = 10⁻²; the value obtained on these data
corresponds to sd ≈ 1.5 × 10⁻⁴, consistent with the pairwise gate correlation
of 0.9995 recorded alongside it.

It is tempting to conclude that the figure therefore measures off-plateau
similarity, and we drew that conclusion in an earlier draft. It does not hold
across implementations. The partition path of an introsort is not specified by
the language, and on the same stored gates, with the same q and the same
formula, two builds of the same library return different figures: 0.9452 to
0.9867 under one and 0.7443 to 0.7659 — the chance level — under another
(Section 4.2.1). The off-plateau values are identical in both. So what the
deterministic figure reflects is the tie order a particular build produces,
which off-plateau similarity influences without determining. A quantity that
moves from near-unity to chance while the data are held fixed is not measuring
the data.

### 4.2.1 The three combinations, measured

Resolving the ties at random on the archived gate — the same gates, the same q,
the same formula, only the tie rule changed — gives 0.7005 +- 0.0068 over 200
draws, against the exact chance value of 0.7012 at that codebase's convention
(q = 28). The reported 0.9418 is reproduced by the deterministic rule **in the
environment in which it was obtained**, and that qualification is not a
formality: the next paragraph is the reason for it.

A note on that chance value, since the two candidates differ by less than our
sampling spread and the choice is therefore easy to get wrong without
consequence. We report the exact hypergeometric expectation
E[J] = sum_k P(k) k/(2q-k), and not the ratio q/(2L-q) that the band-selection
literature uses. The ratio is J evaluated at the expected overlap q^2/L, and
since J(k) = k/(2q-k) is strictly convex in k, Jensen's inequality makes it a
strict lower bound: low by 0.0012 at q = 28 and 0.0009 at q = 29 for a plateau
of 34. The gap changes no verdict reported here, but it is systematic and in
the direction that overstates any excess above chance.

The same configuration holds across the seven training sizes of the revision
data, n = 30 to 1158: the plateau has one distinct value in all 70 runs, and
the decile lies entirely within it. The overlap, however, is not one number.
Because the selected set is decided by the tie rule, it has to be reported
against the rule that produced it, and against the environment where the rule
is not fully specified by the language:

| selection procedure | overlap, seven sizes |
| --- | --- |
| ties broken by band index (`kind="stable"` or `"mergesort"`) | **1.0000** at every size |
| sort's own tie order, numpy 2.4.6 / Python 3.14.6 | 0.9452 to 0.9867 |
| sort's own tie order, numpy 2.5.3 / Python 3.13.16 | 0.7443 to 0.7659 |
| ties resolved at random, 200 draws | 0.7442 at every size |
| exact expectation E[J] for two q-subsets of L | 0.7445 |

Each row is the same stored gates, the same q = 29, the same formula. The third
row is the chance level; the first is unity. Reporting any single one of them as
*the* overlap would be reporting the procedure as if it were the data, and the
2.4.6 row — the historical one, which the published figure and Figure 3 both
carry — is preserved here as the reproduction of a specific procedure in a
specific environment, not as a result another reader will obtain.

We do not claim the library version as the cause. Version, build, array layout
and the partition path all enter an introsort's tie order, and separating them
is not necessary for the conclusion and not attempted here. What the comparison
establishes is that **the Jaccard is a property of the complete selection
procedure, tie rule included, and not an identifiable property of the gates
whenever the selection cuts through a level of exact ties.** That is a stronger
and more precise statement than calling the stability artificial.

It is also a bounded one. It does not make the top-q overlap an invalid
measure in general: where the selection boundary carries no tie — which is the
case in every control arm of Section 6 and in the unanchored arm from n = 200
upward — every tie rule returns the same set and the figure is a measurement
of the gates. What the result says is narrower: in this configuration, with a
quantised prior whose plateau contains the whole decile, the deterministic
value taken on its own does not identify spectral stability. The condition is
checkable before the metric is used, and that is the practical content of the
finding.

The claim is checkable too: `scripts/13_dependencia_do_sort.py` prints the table above with the
environment, the input hashes, the aggregation convention and the selected
index sets.

Resolving the ties at random gives 0.7442 +- 0.0063 in all seven cells, against
an exact expectation of 0.7445 -- a deviation of 0.04 standard deviations. This
is the row that reproduces across environments, because its tie key is random
by construction rather than left to the sort.

That the randomised figure is identical to four decimals across seven training
sizes is not a coincidence of seeding. When the selected set lies entirely
inside a plateau of one distinct value, the statistic is a function of q and the
plateau size alone: **the gate values enter nowhere in it.** This is the sharpest
available statement of C1 with C2. The quantity is not merely driven down to
chance by randomising the ties; under the deterministic rule the gate's
contribution to the selected set is confined to the order the sort leaves, and
no part of it survives a tie rule that ignores that order.

The Raman arm fails C1: its prior is a sum of gaussians with 545 distinct
values and attains its maximum at a single band, so the decile is not
plateau-bound and C2 cannot arise. Section 7 reports it.

| | C1 plateau > q | C2 ties persist | C3 sort resolves | what the figure is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| this work, θ₀ = 0 | yes | no | — | genuine agreement, within one prior level |
| earlier study and its revision data, θ₀ = logit s | yes | yes | yes | the tie rule of the build that ran, from unity to chance |
| Raman, continuous prior | no | no | — | unconstrained by the prior's geometry |

Two distinct statements follow, and conflating them overstates the result. C1
alone bounds **what the metric can register**. C1 with C2 and C3 determines
**the value it returns** — and C3, we can now add, is not a property of the
analysis but of its implementation.

### 4.3 What a reader of the earlier work can and cannot reproduce

One consequence of Section 4.2.1 deserves to be stated plainly rather than
left for a reader to discover, because it concerns the published record and
not only this analysis.

A reader who obtains the archived gates, applies the same selection code with
the same q, and computes the same statistic may not obtain the published
figure. Nothing need have gone wrong: the figure depends on the tie order of
the sort, and that order is not specified by the language where the selected
values are exactly equal. We observed the two outcomes on identical inputs,
and a third rule — tie-breaking by band index — returns unity. The published
number is therefore reproducible in the sense that matters for scrutiny, with
the procedure and environment named, and not reproducible in the sense a
reader would ordinarily assume.

Three practical consequences follow, and they are the ones we would want
applied to our own work:

1. **A top-q overlap should be reported with its tie rule stated**, as a
   matter of course, in the same way a p-value is reported with its test. The
   rule is part of the estimator, not an implementation detail of it.
2. **Where the selection cuts through a level of exact ties, the figure should
   not be reported alone.** The randomised value and the exact expectation
   cost nothing to compute and bound what the deterministic one can mean.
3. **A disagreement between two computations of this statistic need not be
   evidence of error in either — under a condition that must be stated, or
   the point becomes a general excuse for irreproducibility.** It holds when
   the two implement *different* tie rules, or when the rule of the original
   was never specified closely enough to be implemented twice. It does not
   hold when both claim the same procedure on the same inputs under the same
   numerical conditions: there, a divergence still demands investigation, and
   nothing here licenses attributing it to the sort. So the order of enquiry
   is to compare the tie rule and the environment *first*, which is why we
   record the input hashes, q, the aggregation convention and the selected
   index sets and not only the result — and, if those agree, to keep looking.

We make no claim about how widely this affects the band-selection literature,
and we are careful not to make one in the next breath either: whether a
quantised prior with a top-decile cut and a deterministic sort is a common
combination is a question about the literature that we did not study. We have
measured one prior, in one architecture, in two environments. What we can say
is that the three conditions are stated explicitly enough to be checked, and
that checking them on one's own data costs one script and no retraining —
`scripts/13_dependencia_do_sort.py` and `scripts/14_etapa_b_por_procedimento.py`
need only the stored gates and the prior.

### 4.4 What the earlier study already established

The study from which these data come anticipated the possibility that a prior
which immobilises the gate would make reproducibility trivial, and tested it: it
measured the across-seed agreement of the learned displacement d = g_final − g₀
rather than of the final gate, obtaining ρ(d) = 0.9462 in NIR and 0.976 in
Raman, and reported that the agreement on *which* bands move most is
considerably weaker, with a top-decile overlap of 0.38 in NIR. That control uses
gate values over all 281 bands rather than ranks within a tied block, and is
therefore immune to the artefact described here: no selection operator, no
top-q cut, and no tie-breaking rule enters it. Immunity to that artefact is
not independence from the prior's plateau, and the two should not be read as
one. Under prior-initialisation the plateau's displacement is the same to
within about a millionth across seeds, so twelve per cent of the spectrum
contributes agreement to that correlation for a reason unrelated to learning.
Section 9.3 measures how much: excluding the plateau lowers the correlation by
0.018 under Pearson and 0.051 under Spearman at the largest training size, and
by considerably more in small samples.

Our result is accordingly narrow and additive. It concerns what the final-gate
top-decile overlap registers under a quantised prior — not the arithmetic of any
reported value, which we reproduce exactly, and not the conclusion that control
supports. Gate correlations and predictive R² are likewise unaffected, since
both use values rather than their order.

### 4.5 Scope

The two statements of Section 4.2 have different scopes. C1 — a plateau larger
than q — is a property of the prior and the decile size alone, and bounds what
any top-q overlap on that prior can register. C2 and C3 concern a particular
initialisation and a particular tie-breaking rule, and determine the value the
statistic returns. Neither is a claim about top-k overlap in general, and
neither is a claim about other forms of interpretive stability: a measure may
legitimately be insensitive to sample size, or legitimately high, for reasons
that have nothing to do with the three conditions here. The Raman arm used a
continuous prior, fails C1, and is treated in Section 7.

It is worth separating what this paper establishes at three different strengths,
because they are easy to read as one claim and only two of them are results.

| | status |
| --- | --- |
| **The computational mechanism.** Where the selection boundary cuts through a level of exactly equal values, the top-q set is decided by the tie rule rather than by the values. | **Demonstrated**, and not empirically: it follows from what a sort does with equal keys. The boundary condition is checkable on any stored gate. |
| **The experimental consequence.** On the near-infrared gates examined here, procedures differing only in tie rule return overlaps from 0.7442 to 1.0000 on identical inputs, and the published figure is one of them. | **Observed**, in this dataset, this architecture, this prior, two environments. |
| **The general prevalence.** How large and how frequent this effect is across other architectures, priors and datasets. | **Not established.** We did not measure it, and nothing here should be read as having measured it. It is an implication worth investigating, offered as a question rather than a finding. |

The contribution of this paper is the first two rows. The third is stated so
that a reader does not take it from us, and the check that would begin to
answer it is in the repository and needs no retraining.

## 5. Results — the response to sample size

The two conditions of the revision data separate on a property that requires no
knowledge of the mechanism — and, it turns out, one of them separates
identically under every selection procedure while the other has no single
answer to give.

**Without anchoring, the overlap rises with sample size, and the rise does not
depend on the tie rule.** It reaches 0.5658 at n = 1158 from roughly a tenth at
n = 30, and the rank correlation with n is **+0.9286 under index-order
tie-breaking, under the sort's own tie order, and under randomisation alike**.
The three procedures agree because, from n = 200 upward, no tie group sits on
the selection boundary at rank q, and a rule can only reorder values that are
exactly equal.

**Under prior-initialisation there is no association to report.** The series is
constant at 1.0000 under index-order tie-breaking and constant at 0.7442 under
randomisation; a rank correlation is undefined on a constant, which is a result
and not a missing value. The series varies only under the sort's own tie order,
and there what varies is the tie order: −0.3214 in the environment of the
published analysis and +0.5714 in a second environment, on the same stored
gates. The historical −0.3214 is retained as that environment's figure and is
not a property of the gates.

**The move from −0.3214 to +0.5714 is not a change in the relation between
spectral stability and sample size.** No such relation changed; the data are
the same bytes. What the two coefficients show is that a correlation computed
from an implicit deterministic selection is a function of the implementation
that resolved the ties. Reading the sign flip as evidence about stability and
sample size would repeat, one level up, the error the section is about.

Both associations are **exploratory**. The seven sizes are nested subsamples of
one collection of 725 fruits and the ten runs within a size share that size's
training data, so these are not seven independent draws from a population of
datasets; the p-values that accompanied these coefficients in earlier drafts
(0.0025 and 0.4821) assumed a replication unit the design does not supply, and
we report the coefficients descriptively and drop the p-values rather than
re-deriving a justification we do not have.

We draw the narrow conclusion: the two conditions do not measure the same
quantity. We do not claim that a legitimate stability measure must depend on n
— a measure may be flat because selection has already stabilised, because the
signal is too weak for more data to resolve, or because the procedure is
insensitive to the data by construction. Insensitivity to a large change in n
is a reason to look for the cause, and here the cause is measured.

The unanchored arm is not wholly tie-free, and the distinction matters for how
the comparison should be read. The criterion is not whether ties exist but
whether one sits **on the boundary** at rank q: ties strictly inside the
selected set leave the set unchanged, and only a group spanning the boundary
lets the rule decide which of its members are taken. From n = 200 upward no
group spans it — at n = 700 two tied values sit inside the selected set and
change nothing — and every procedure agrees to every digit. Below that, some
seeds end training with two or three distinct values across the 34 bands of the
plateau region, and the deterministic rule gains up to 0.028 over the randomised
one (n = 100: 0.1663 against 0.1393; both decile conventions agree on this).
That residual is two orders of magnitude below the 0.22 the anchored arm loses,
and the selected set there is not plateau-bound (0.000 to 0.276 of it falls
inside the plateau, against 1.000 when the prior initialises the gate). The
unanchored condition therefore measures band selection, with a small tie
artefact at the smallest sample sizes; the anchored condition, in NIR, does
not measure it at all.

## 6. Results — the prior controls

None of the metrics we tested separates the prior's chemical content from the
mere fact of anchoring. The pre-registered hypothesis that anchoring alone
buys little was not supported in any partition, in either arm: anchoring on a
prior whose content has been destroyed raises agreement with the separate
statistical reference from +0.031…+0.070 at λ = 0 to +0.087…+0.464 at λ = 1, so the
anchoring term and the content term have to be reported as separate
contributions rather than as one effect. Reproducibility behaves the same way
and survives its criterion by the wrong route. No control arm beats the
literature prior on seed-to-seed overlap — all ten control differences are
negative or null — but the `shuffled` arm, whose prior correlates *negatively*
with the data-derived reference in four of five partitions, still reaches an
overlap of 0.957 to 0.975 against the literature prior's 0.9763 to 1.0000.
Stability across seeds is therefore attainable with a prior anticorrelated
with the reference, which is enough to show that stability does not validate
the interpretation it is usually offered to support. Table 1 gives the
verdicts for all five control hypotheses.

These overlaps are not the quantity Section 4 found uninterpretable, and the
difference must be stated or this section would appear to contradict that one.
All runs reported here use θ₀ = 0, so no gate parameter is saturated, the count
of inert bands is zero in every cell, and the analysis script verifies the
absence of exact ties among the sorted values before reporting any overlap.
These figures are therefore genuine agreements among distinguishable values, at
q = 29.

That last point is the one Section 4 makes load-bearing, so we checked it
rather than inferred it. No tie group sits on the selection boundary at rank q
in any run of this arm, and a tie rule can only reorder values that are exactly
equal — so index-order tie-breaking, the sort's own order and randomised
tie-breaking return **the same figure to every digit** here. On the versioned
control artefacts at λ = 1 the three procedures all give 0.9867, and the same
holds in the partition directories from which the range above is computed.
The invariance is arithmetic rather than a judgement: with nothing tied at the
boundary there is nothing for a rule to decide. These numbers consequently
need no qualification by sort kind, which is precisely what distinguishes them
from Section 4's.

Two things make that statement checkable rather than asserted.
`scripts/14_etapa_b_por_procedimento.py` tests the boundary condition in every
directory given to it and ends with a single verdict line naming any directory
where a tie group spans the boundary, where the procedures disagree, or which
was not measured at all. And the condition is sufficient for what we need from
it without being stronger than necessary: absence of ties across the whole
spectrum is not required, only that no tie group spans the selection boundary,
and that the ordering is over the same values with the rule reordering equal
values alone. The 0.9418 of Section 4 comes from a different configuration — the
prior in the initialisation, at q = 28 — and the two must not be compared
directly. What makes the controls damaging to the interpretation here is not
that the numbers are artefacts; it is that a prior anticorrelated with the
reference earns a genuine one.

The pattern is not uniform across partitions, and any claim about it has to
say so. On the raw agreement metric the literature prior beats the relocated
`blocks` control in **4 of the 15** near-infrared partitions — one of the first
five, none of the second five, and three of the last five. We report the
aggregate and the per-block counts together because the aggregate alone would
support either a positive or a negative reading depending on which five
partitions a reader had seen.

## 7. Results — the Raman control

This section is where the explanation offered in Sections 3 and 4 is put at
risk. If the behaviour we describe followed from the architecture, the gate, or
the objective, it would appear again on a second modality. If it follows from
the prior's quantisation, it should disappear when the prior's functional form
changes and nothing else does. The code, the gate, the penalty and the training
procedure are identical between the two arms; only the prior differs.

### 7.1 The two conditions are absent

The Raman prior is a sum of Gaussians rather than a set of constants per
region. Over 1870 Raman shifts it takes 545 distinct values and attains its
maximum at a single band, with 1326 bands resting on its lower clip.

**The selected set is not confined to a tied block.** The fraction of the top
decile falling inside the 1326-band floor is 0.000 in all three partitions, and
the fraction inside the one-band maximum is 0.005 — one band of 187. The
corresponding fraction in near-infrared is 1.000 in 15 of 15 cells. The first
condition of Section 4 is not met.

**There is no tie-breaking collapse.** The trained gates carry 5, 4 and 8
exactly tied values among 1870 bands. The overlap with ties left to the sort is
0.9596, 0.9589 and 0.9303; resolving those ties at random returns the same
three values, to a difference of 0.0000 in all three partitions. The
near-infrared arm of the earlier study drops by 0.2413 under the same
operation, and the seven training sizes of its revision data drop by the same
order. The second and third conditions are not met either.

Both of the properties documented in Sections 4 and 5 are therefore absent
under the same architecture and the same code, with only the functional form of
the prior exchanged. This is what the quantisation account predicts, and it is
the strongest control this work has: the behaviour is a property of how the
prior was written down, not of the method.

### 7.2 Anchoring buys reproducibility here, and the figure means it

With the defect absent, the same comparison becomes informative. Without
anchoring the Raman overlap is 0.56 to 0.58; with anchoring it rises to 0.93 to
0.98. There is no tied block to inflate it and no sort order inside the
selected set, so this increase is a measurement of what anchoring does to
selection stability rather than an artefact of the metric. The contrast with
near-infrared is the point of reporting both: the same nominal gain means
different things in the two arms, and only the geometry of the prior tells them
apart.

### 7.3 The strongest supporting fact is not in any verdict

One quantity in this arm requires no model at all. Correlating the prior's own
shape with the data-derived reference, before any training:

| arm | partition 0 | partition 1 | partition 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| literature | **+0.2390** | **+0.1660** | **+0.1600** |
| `blocks` (relocated) | +0.0064 | −0.0435 | +0.0533 |
| `shuffled` | −0.0045 | −0.0141 | +0.0190 |

The published Raman prior is positioned where the data-derived reference puts
weight, and relocating or shuffling it drives that correlation to zero. This is
the opposite of what the near-infrared controls show, it is obtained without
fitting the gated model, and it is the clearest evidence in this paper that a
literature prior can carry information about the signal. It appears in no
pre-registered verdict, because we did not think to pre-register it.

### 7.4 The between-arm comparison, with its reversal

The pre-registered comparison asked whether the literature prior beats the
relocated control on agreement with the reference, with a criterion of two of
three partitions:

| partition | literature | `blocks` | delta | p |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | +0.1964 | +0.0899 | **+0.1065** | 0.0020 |
| 1 | +0.1855 | +0.0855 | **+0.1000** | 0.0020 |
| 2 | +0.1584 | +0.1660 | **−0.0075** | 0.0098 |

The criterion is met at 2 of 3, but partition 2 is a *significant* reversal,
not a near-miss, and reporting the verdict without it would misdescribe the
result. The two supporting margins are about thirteen times larger than the
contrary one, which is a statement about magnitude and not about evidence: at
p = 0.0098 the test detected the reversal, and calling a detected difference a
tie would contradict our own test. **The statement the data support is
therefore: the literature prior wins clearly in two of three partitions, and in
the third the relocated control holds a small advantage that the test
registers.** It is not "the literature prior wins in Raman" without
qualification, and it is not a tie; we write it neither way anywhere in this
paper. As everywhere else here, what that p-value licenses depends on the test
matching the unit of replication, and the three Raman partitions are splits of
one collection (Section 2.7).

## 8. Results — where the alignment is

The gate does follow the data. This section reports where it can be seen to,
and the place is not the one the top-decile metric reads.

### 8.1 Alignment at the prior's floor, and what the plateau does not show

Within each level of the step-function prior, we correlate the gate's values
with the separate statistical reference across the bands at that level. This asks whether
the gate orders bands that the prior treats as equivalent, which is the only
place the gate can contribute information the prior did not already supply.
The two levels large enough for the analysis to interpret are the floor, with
223 bands, and the plateau, with 34; the remaining level of 7 bands is below
the threshold and is measured but never interpreted.

At the prior's floor — the level of 0.1, carrying 223 of the 281 bands — the
correlation is positive in every partition measured: +0.2982, +0.1679, +0.1457,
+0.3418 and +0.2147 across partitions 5 to 9, and +0.2705, +0.3168, +0.1491,
+0.2901 and +0.2063 across partitions 10 to 14. In each of the ten, the
pre-registered test over the ten random initialisations within that partition
reaches its smallest attainable two-sided value, p = 0.0020. The first group of
five was pre-registered as a secondary observation and the second as a primary
criterion, and the direction is the same in all ten.

At the prior's maximum — the 34-band plateau — the pattern is different. The
partition means there are negative in four of the ten, two in each group of
five, and range from −0.1495 to +0.3578 against the floor's +0.1457 to
+0.3418. The descriptive pattern recurred in both groups of five partitions:
two negative means in each, with group means of +0.1139 and +0.1145.

**What may and may not be concluded from this needs stating carefully, and
three separate limits apply.**

*First, the partitions are not independent replicates.* The ten partitions are
repeated splits of the same 725 fruits, with approximately 80% overlap between
training sets (78% to 82% over the 45 pairs). Consequently, the
partition-level Wilcoxon p-values cannot be interpreted under the usual
independence assumption. We report them descriptively — p = 0.0020 for the
floor against zero, p = 0.1055 for the plateau, and p = 0.3223 for the two
paired against each other — but draw no population-level inference from them.
They are the output of tests whose inferential interpretation is compromised
by the design, not descriptive statistics, and we identify them as such
wherever they appear. The dependence is positive, which suggests the nominal
levels are optimistic, but the magnitude and even the direction of the
distortion depend on the joint distribution of these statistics, which we have
not characterised; we therefore do not assert that the tests are
anti-conservative, only that they are not calibrated for this design.

The pre-registered criterion for this result is a different test, within each
partition and across the ten random initialisations. That criterion is also
bounded, and the bound belongs in the methods rather than in a footnote: the
ten initialisations share the same training data, so the test characterises
the variability of the optimisation procedure conditional on the observed
partition. It does not, on its own, support population-level inference about
spectral relevance.

*Second, failing to clear a criterion is not evidence of absence.* The
plateau's correlations do not establish a positive association there; they also
do not establish that the gate carries no ordering information at that level.
Four of ten partition means are negative and six are positive, which is a
pattern we describe and do not interpret as an absence.

*Third, we did not demonstrate a difference between the two levels, and that
is not a demonstration that they are the same.* A paired comparison over the
partitions does not reach significance, which is not an equivalence test and
licenses no claim that the levels behave alike. A difference in significance
is not the significance of a difference, and the converse error is equally
available.

What survives all three limits is the description: the floor's partition means
are positive in ten of ten, the plateau's are not, and the selection metric of
Section 4 reads only the plateau.

### 8.2 The alignment does not depend on which prior was used

The same within-level correlation, measured on the control arms, gives +0.12 to
+0.37 across all three. A prior whose content has been destroyed produces the
same alignment as the published one. Whatever the gate is recovering at the
prior's floor, it is recovering from the data, and the prior's identity does not
enter.

This is the result that constrains the interpretation most tightly, and it cuts
both ways. Against the reading we began with, it shows that the alignment is
not evidence for the literature prior in particular. In favour of the method,
it shows the gate is not inert and not merely reproducing its initialisation:
something data-dependent is happening, consistently, in the 223 bands at the
prior's floor. Whether the same is true at the plateau is not settled by these
measurements in either direction.

### 8.3 What this is not

**This is not evidence that the gate recovers true chemical relevance.** The
reference is the vector of absolute coefficients of a partial least squares
model calibrated on the same training partition. It is an operational
reference — a second statistical estimate from the same data, not an
independent determination of which wavelengths carry dry-matter information.
Agreement between the gate and that reference means the two methods weight
similar regions. It does not establish that either is right, and it cannot: no
ground-truth support exists for this dataset, which is the gap identified in
Section 1.1 and the first item of the future work in Section 11.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 A fit–constraint trade-off that does not bind

The penalty weight λ is meant to trade fit against adherence to the prior, and
in the usual telling λ → ∞ is high bias while λ → 0 is high variance. Neither
end of that description survives measurement here, because the regularisation
path is **flat in risk**: held-out R² is equivalent across the whole range of
λ, in both modalities, and at all seven training sizes of the revision data.
Nothing is being traded. What varies along the path is the reproducibility of
the gate, and Sections 4 through 6 establish that the reproducibility in the
near-infrared arm is either uninterpretable or attainable with a prior
anticorrelated with the reference. The trade-off, stated honestly, is between
a stability that does not affect predictive risk and nothing at all.

This is not an argument against spectral priors. It is an argument against
reading a flat risk path as evidence that the prior costs nothing while the
interpretive metric improves, when the metric's improvement is the part that
does not survive scrutiny.

### 9.2 The objective resembles a PAC-Bayes bound and is not one

The trained objective is an empirical risk plus a Kullback–Leibler divergence
between the gate and a prior specified before seeing the data, which is the
shape of a PAC-Bayes bound. The resemblance is formal and we draw nothing from
it. Three things would have to change for the analogy to become an analysis:
the gate would have to be stochastic rather than a point estimate, so that
there is a posterior over hypotheses for the divergence to be taken between;
the divergence would have to be between distributions over hypotheses rather
than between a deterministic gate and a fixed vector; and inference would have
to sample from that posterior rather than evaluate a single gate.

We state this because the resemblance invites a generalisation claim that the
objective does not license, and because we made that error ourselves during
this work: we briefly treated an observed near-zero difference in held-out R²
as a prediction that a bound would contradict, when the objective being
optimised is not a bound and therefore predicts nothing of the kind. A
stochastic gate is listed in Section 11 as the change that would make the
analysis available.

### 9.3 Why the displacement control is immune, and what we have not measured

The earlier study anticipated the objection that a prior might buy
reproducibility by immobilising the gate rather than by guiding it, and
answered it with a displacement control: it measures d = g_final − g₀, the
change the gate undergoes during training, and reports the reproducibility of
that change across seeds, together with the observation that band-level
identification from |d| is weak. That control is what supports its interpretive
claim, and the defect documented in this paper does not reach it. The reason is
structural and worth setting out, because a reader who has followed Section 4
will reasonably ask.

The defect of Section 4 requires three things: a selected set of fixed size q,
a tied block larger than q from which that set is drawn, and a rule that
resolves the ties. The displacement correlation has none of them. It applies no
selection operator, so there is no q and no top-k cut; it is computed over all
281 bands rather than over a subset; and it therefore never consults an
ordering within a tied block, so no tie-breaking rule enters and `argsort`'s
instability cannot propagate into it. Each of the three conditions fails
separately, and any one failure is sufficient.

The statement we make is the author's:

> The displacement-based control is immune to the deterministic top-k
> tie-breaking artefact documented here, because it operates on gate
> displacements across all spectral bands without selecting a fixed-size
> subset. This does not imply immunity to prior-induced effects: under
> prior-based initialization, the saturated plateau contributes structurally
> identical, near-zero displacements across runs. The influence of these
> coordinates on displacement correlation must therefore be assessed
> separately.

### 9.3.1 The dependence, measured

Under the prior in the initialisation, θ₀ = logit(s), so g₀ = σ(logit(s)) = s
exactly, and d = g_final − s. Three facts about the plateau have to be kept
apart, because only the first is an exact equality and the paper has conflated
them before.

**Within each run, the gate values on the plateau are exactly equal to one
another.** Each seed holds one distinct value over the 34 bands, identical both
by value and by bit pattern in 10 of 10 runs in each of the seven training
sizes, and it is that equality *inside a run* that leaves the selected set to a
tie-breaking rule. This is the artefact of Section 4, and it is the only
property the artefact needs.

**Across runs, the common plateau value is not the same.** We measured this
separately, per training size and never pooled across them, and it differs in
every one: the amplitude of the common value over the ten seeds runs from
2.4 × 10⁻⁷ at n = 30 to 4.1 × 10⁻⁶ at n = 1158. It is larger at larger sample
sizes over these seven conditions, which we report as an exploratory
association and not as evidence that more data drives seeds apart; seven
conditions cannot support that. So the two properties come apart, and the
mechanism rests on the first: a tie inside a run does not require two runs to
tie with each other.

**The displacement on the plateau is numerically negligible but not zero.** The
largest |d| there is between 1.2 × 10⁻⁶ and 6.7 × 10⁻⁶ across the seven
training sizes, against 1.6 × 10⁻² to 5.3 × 10⁻² under θ₀ = 0. The gate did
move, by about a part in a million. Saying d = 0 would be convenient and
false.

**What follows for the correlation is a near-constant block.** Under
prior-initialisation every plateau displacement lies within 6.7 × 10⁻⁶ of zero,
and the seeds differ there by between 2.4 × 10⁻⁷ and 4.1 × 10⁻⁶ depending on
the training size. Twelve per cent of the spectrum therefore carries a
displacement that is the same between seeds to within a few parts in a million,
and that near-constancy contributes agreement to ρ(d) for a reason unrelated to
learning. The dependence requires neither an exact zero nor exact equality
across seeds, which is why the measured near-constancy suffices.

Two caveats on the resolution of these figures.

The first is where the resolution is read. A gate is σ(θ) < 1, so the plateau
sits just below one, inside the binade [0.5, 1), where single-precision values
are spaced 2⁻²⁴ ≈ 5.96 × 10⁻⁸ apart. The familiar 2⁻²³ applies immediately
*above* one: unity is a power of two and lies on the boundary between two
spacing regions. At the correct local spacing, the across-run amplitudes are 4,
6, 4, 6, 20, 34 and 68 steps across the seven sizes — integer multiples, which
is what makes the figures look quantised. The resolution is a property of the
region the values occupy and not a universal detection floor for this quantity.

The second is what that quantisation licenses. The stored values are exactly
representable in single precision, which is a fact about where they lie and
not about how they were produced: rounding, an explicit cast, or a
quantisation step before storage would leave the same trace. We therefore
report the representability as measured, offer single-precision arithmetic
somewhere upstream as the explanation we find likeliest, and do not claim the
provenance, which would require tracing the types used in training, export and
loading. We

the correlation by −0.024 to +0.003, a mean of −0.012, against a mean of
−0.114 in the anchored arm — about nine times smaller. The premise is
confirmed directly, the largest |d| on the plateau being between 1.2 × 10⁻⁶
and 6.7 × 10⁻⁶ under prior-initialisation against 1.6 × 10⁻² to 5.3 × 10⁻²
under θ₀ = 0.

### 9.3.2 Its magnitude, and what the response to n does and does not show

Two things follow, and they point in opposite directions.

The dependence is largest where the published control makes no claim. At
n ≤ 100 the correlation outside the plateau is 0.04 to 0.14 against a total of
0.21 to 0.26, so displacement reproducibility in that regime is mostly the
plateau. At the full training size it is small: 0.9369 against 0.9185 under
Pearson and 0.8879 against 0.8369 under Spearman, a contribution of order 0.02
to 0.05. **Both definitions give the same answer, so the unrecorded definition
of the published figure does not change this conclusion.**

And the displacement correlation responds to the data in the way the decile
overlap does not. Its rank correlation with training size is +0.9643 over all
bands and +0.8929 with the plateau removed. The decile overlap under the same
initialisation has no comparable figure to offer: its series is constant under
index-order tie-breaking and constant under randomised tie-breaking, so a rank
correlation is undefined, and it varies only under the sort's own tie order,
where it gave −0.3214 in the environment of the published analysis and +0.5714
in another (Section 4.2.1). The contrast therefore holds more sharply than a
comparison of two coefficients would suggest: one quantity responds to the
data, and the other has no response to report that is a property of the gates.

We give these rank correlations without p-values. The seven sizes are nested
subsamples of one collection and the runs within a size share its training
data, so the replication unit the test would need is not available here
(Section 5); the coefficients are descriptive.

Two cautions on reading that, both of which we judge necessary. The rank
correlation establishes a strong monotonic association between displacement
reproducibility and training size across the seven conditions examined, and
nothing beyond it. It does not establish that what remains once the plateau is
removed is the learning of chemically relevant bands: that inference needs a
known support, and neither dataset here provides one (Sections 1.1 and 8.3).
And responding to sample size is a diagnostic of sensitivity to the data, not
a test of interpretive validity. What we may say is that the displacement
control is sensitive to the data where the decile overlap is not, which is
favourable evidence about the measure and not a validation of any
interpretation built on it.

The conclusion therefore has three parts and we state all three. The
displacement control is immune to the artefact this paper documents. It
carries a measurable dependence on the prior's plateau: excluding the plateau
bands lowers the correlation by 0.018 under Pearson and 0.051 under Spearman
at the largest sample size, and by considerably more in small samples. What
survives that exclusion remains associated with sample size. None of this
amounts to a demonstration that the gate recovers chemically relevant bands,
and we do not offer it as one.

### 9.4 Prescriptions

Five recommendations follow, in increasing order of what they cost.

1. **Check all three conditions together, not the geometry alone.** Compare
   the prior's largest level set against q; check whether exact ties among the
   gate values survive training; and check what rule resolves them. Each is one
   line. A level set larger than q makes the artefact *possible*, because the
   selection can then fall entirely inside one level — but it does not by
   itself mean the metric registers nothing the prior does not already make,
   since the gate may separate those bands by differences it genuinely learned.
   What the figure reports is a tie rule's output only when all three hold, and
   the three are what Section 4.2 separates.
2. **Report the overlap under randomised tie resolution alongside the
   conventional figure.** The difference between the two is the size of the
   artefact, it costs one line of code, and it requires no change to the
   training procedure.
3. **Report the exact chance expectation, not q/(2L − q).** The ratio is J
   evaluated at the expected overlap and understates the expectation, in the
   direction that overstates any excess above chance (Section 2.5).
4. **Where the gate's band ordering is to be interpreted, avoid an
   initialisation that leaves parameters saturated — or demonstrate that it
   does not.** This is a methodological preference conditional on the use, not
   a general prohibition: nothing here shows that initialising at a prior is
   wrong for every purpose. In the configuration we examined, θ₀ = 0 with the
   prior in the penalty preserves the constraint while leaving every parameter
   reachable, and removes the condition of Section 4 entirely (Section 3.2);
   held-out R² is equivalent between the two across the λ grid, both
   modalities and the seven training sizes we ran, which is the evidence for
   the cost being nil and is as general as those runs. Section 9.3 adds a
   second reason within the same scope: initialising at the prior also leaves
   a block of coordinates with near-zero displacement, which contaminates a
   displacement-based control that would otherwise be clean.
5. **Vary the training set size and check whether the overlap responds.**
   Insensitivity across a large change in n is a reason to look for a cause,
   not a finding in itself — see the limitation in Section 10.

These are ordered by cost, which is not the same as being ordered by what each
one settles, and the difference matters enough to state. Inspecting the
selection boundary for ties (recommendation 1) **identifies the possibility of
tie-dependence directly**, by arithmetic on stored values. Specifying and
comparing tie rules (2) **quantifies** how much the selection depends on it.
Separating initialisation from regularisation (4) investigates the mechanism by
which the prior exerts its influence. Varying n (5) examines a complementary
experimental response — how the selection behaves as the quantity of data
changes — and it answers a different question rather than the same one less
well. **It does not substitute for the boundary inspection**: an overlap that
fails to respond to n is consistent with tie-dependence and with several other
causes, while a tie group spanning the boundary settles the matter in one
line and no retraining.

Conditioning the metric on the prior is not a substitute for any of these, and
Section 10 records why.

## 10. Limitations

**The reference is operational, not chemical truth.** Every agreement figure in
this paper compares the gate against the vector of absolute coefficients of a
partial least squares model calibrated on the same training partition. It is a
second statistical estimate from the same data. No ground-truth band support
exists for either dataset, so nothing here measures recovery of chemical
relevance, only concordance between two estimators.

**No accuracy claim for Raman.** The Raman ingestion exposes no batch
identifier, so no grouping variable is available and the partitions are not
grouped. Held-out R² is therefore not reported for that modality, and the
Raman arm functions exclusively as a control on the geometry of the prior.

**Two pre-registered criteria measured something other than what they were
written to measure, and we kept both verdicts.** The first conditioned the
agreement metric on the prior in order to test the prior's content; because the
prior is the variable that distinguishes the arms, conditioning on it removes
the contrast and rewards a prior anticorrelated with the reference. The
criterion was badly designed, the verdict stands as recorded, and the reading
we had attached to it is withdrawn. The second tested whether a residual decile
leaves the plateau, which it does — but leaving the plateau was not the same as
being unconfined, and the decile went to the prior's floor. Both are reported
in Table 1 with the verdict and the later interpretation in separate columns.

**One joint reading was never specified.** Two hypotheses about within-level
ordering were pre-registered separately, and how to read them together was not.
We report them separately and do not combine them.

**The historical top-decile overlap results are conditional on the selection
procedure used to resolve tied gate values.** In the examined near-infrared
experiments, different sorting implementations produced different overlap
values from identical stored gates when ties crossed the top-q boundary.
Consequently, reproducing the gate values alone does not guarantee
reproduction of the reported overlap. The tie-breaking rule, the value of q,
and the relevant computational implementation must also be specified. This
limitation applies to the affected selection results and should not be
generalised to experiments in which the selection boundary contains no ties.
It is a limitation of how the results were documented and can be interpreted,
not a further finding: the finding is in Section 4.2.1, and it is that the
selection can depend on the tie rule at all.

**The response to sample size is an observation about this experiment, not a
rule.** In the examined sample-size experiment, unanchored overlap increased
with n. For the prior-initialised arm, index-based and randomised
tie-breaking produced constant overlap series, for which a rank correlation
with n is undefined; correlations obtained under implementation-dependent
sorting therefore cannot be interpreted as evidence of a sample-size response
of the learned gates. We
do not claim that a legitimate stability measure must depend on n: a measure
may be flat because selection has already stabilised, because the signal is too
weak for more data to resolve, or because the procedure is insensitive to the
data by construction. What the data support is the narrower statement that the
two conditions do not measure the same quantity.

**Fruit identity is inferred, so the leakage check is bounded by that
inference.** Absence of leakage was verified against fruit identities inferred
from the available records, not against independent physical identifiers. The
published file carries no fruit column; the grouping is constructed from runs
of consecutive rows with identical metadata and dry-matter value, and the
verification asks whether any such key appears on both sides of a split. It
does not, in any of the splits we use. Concluding from this that no physical
fruit straddles a split requires assuming that every measurement of one fruit
carries an identical key — the assumption the construction itself rests on —
so the check cannot be independent of it. The observable structure is
consistent with the recorded 725 fruits, 723 of them measured twice, which
makes the inference plausible without making it verified. Section 2.1.

**The PAC-Bayes connection is a formal resemblance.** Section 9.2.

**Displacement reproducibility is not interpretable in small samples under
prior-initialisation.** Section 9.3 reports that at n ≤ 100 the displacement
correlation outside the prior's plateau is 0.04 to 0.14 against a total of
0.21 to 0.26, so most of what the measure registers there is the block of
coordinates held at zero displacement by the initialisation. At the full
training size the contribution falls to between 0.02 and 0.05. We report the
figure with its sample size for this reason, and the published control, which
is computed at full size, is not affected by the small-sample behaviour. The
definition of the correlation used in that published control is not recorded
in our materials. Both Pearson and Spearman give the same qualitative
conclusion here, so the omission does not affect what we claim -- but the
original definition is still required to reproduce the earlier figure exactly,
and we record it as an open methodological point rather than a resolved one.

**The programme carries two decile conventions.** The stored metrics of the
earlier revision and this work use `ceil(0.10 p)` = 29; the published ablation
uses `floor(p/10)` = 28. They give different figures and different chance
levels, they are declared per protocol in Section 2.4, and they are never
mixed. This is an inconsistency in the programme, not a divergence between
sources: the two numbers that appeared to disagree were each correct under
their own convention. No figure is rehabilitated by the clarification, because
a tied block larger than q makes the selection figure a tie-breaking artefact
at either q.

**Five mechanical explanations were proposed during this work and discarded.**
Each was advanced as the cause of an observation and then refuted by a further
measurement — in one case by a synthetic fixture that was itself miscalibrated,
which reversed a correct finding before the real re-run reinstated it. We
record this because a reader is entitled to know how much of the final account
was arrived at by elimination rather than by prediction.

## 11. Future work

**A known support.** Every agreement figure in this paper is concordance with
a proxy. Measuring recovery rather than concordance requires a design in which
the informative bands are known independently of the model: synthetic spectra
with a support fixed by construction are the cheapest such design, and
controlled mixtures, reference standards and experimental systems whose
composition is set by the experimenter are others. We name synthetic data
because it is what we would run first, not because it is the only route. This is the first item because it is the one that would
convert the interpretive claim from unfalsifiable to testable.

**Empirical Rademacher complexity by fitting random labels.** Section 1.2
argues that prior-initialisation restricts reachability and not capacity. That
argument is analytic. Fitting random labels under both initialisations would
quantify the restriction instead of arguing it, and would test whether the
frozen plateau has any effect on what the model can fit.

**A stochastic gate.** The change that would make a PAC-Bayes analysis
available rather than a formal resemblance (Section 9.2).

**Scale and small samples.** The prior does not improve accuracy at any of
seven training sizes from n = 30 to 1158. That is a real result from the
earlier revision data, it bears directly on when a prior is worth using, and it
has no published home.

**The number of latent components against within-plateau ordering.** Across the
five partitions where both were measured, the rank correlation between the
selected H and the within-plateau agreement is +0.6000, with n = 5 and
p = 0.2848. Five points cannot settle it. We name it, declare the number, and
state that we did not run it.

## 12. Conclusions

This study examined how prior-guided spectral gating can produce apparent
feature-selection stability that is not necessarily attributable to
data-driven spectral discrimination. By separating the roles of prior-based
initialisation and regularisation, we identified conditions under which the
prior's influence is not merely strong but inert in practice: where the prior
saturates the gate, the affected parameters receive gradient of order 10⁻⁶,
the penalty weight ceases to move them over four orders of magnitude, and the
bands held there are the ones the prior declares most important. The plateau
parameters exhibit small but nonzero changes, with maximum displacements
between 1.2 × 10⁻⁶ and 6.7 × 10⁻⁶ across the examined training sizes,
indicating operational rather than mathematical immobility. This limits the interpretation of a stable top-q selection as
evidence of data-driven selection. It does not limit the interpretation of the
gate as a whole: outside the saturated level the gate aligns positively with
a separate statistical reference in every partition measured, that alignment
is the same under a prior whose content has been destroyed, and it occurs at
the level of the prior that carries most of the spectrum and that the
top-decile metric never reads. That reference is separate from the gate's own
training but not independent of the data: it is a second statistical estimate
built on the same training partition, so the alignment is evidence of learned
structure associated with the data and not a demonstration that the gate
recovers chemically true bands.

A second finding concerns the measurement of selection stability itself. When
the top-q boundary intersects a group of exactly tied gate values, the
selected subset depends on the tie-breaking rule. Consequently, Jaccard
overlap is a property of the complete selection procedure, rather than an
independently identifiable property of the gate values. In the examined
near-infrared experiments, identical stored gates produced substantially
different overlap values under different sorting procedures and
implementations. Where no tied group crossed the selection boundary — every
control arm at θ₀ = 0, and the unanchored arm from n = 200 upward — the
selected subsets were invariant to tie-breaking, and we verified this rather
than assuming it.

These findings establish a computational mechanism and document its
experimental consequences under the investigated architecture, prior and
datasets. They do not establish how frequently the mechanism occurs in other
spectral-learning systems. The results therefore support a specific
methodological requirement: claims of spectral-selection stability should
identify the selection procedure and verify whether ties intersect the
selection boundary before overlap is interpreted as evidence of reproducible
feature selection. Both checks are arithmetic on quantities already stored,
and neither requires retraining.

Taken together, the two findings point at one thing rather than two. Apparent
stability of a spectral band selection can arise from the interaction between
the prior knowledge built into the model, the behaviour of the gates, and the
procedure that turns those gates into a discrete selection. This paper
demonstrates specific mechanisms of that interaction. How common they are
elsewhere is a question we leave open, and one that the checks above make
cheap to ask.
